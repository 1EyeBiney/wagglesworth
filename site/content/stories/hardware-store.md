Greetings, commoners. It is I, Baroness Wagglesworth, Supreme Ruler of Fabulousness and—on this particular day—Assistant Director of Hardware Procurement.

Dad announced that we were going to the hardware store, or as he calls it, “the best store in the world.”

I found this difficult to believe.

There were no clothes. No shoes. No salon. The only snack Dad offered was beef jerky, and even that came with the insulting phrase, “just one piece.”

Still, a Baroness must occasionally inspect the working conditions of her kingdom.

Because Dad is blind, Uncle Chris came along to help us find the things on Dad’s list. Dad carried his cane, Uncle Chris pushed the cart, and I supervised from the front like a fuzzy golden project manager.

Before entering, Dad drank a chai tea.

“You know,” he said, “some people bring a list to the hardware store.”

I gave him a look.

I had a list. It simply included everything.

Inside, the building was enormous. The aisles stretched farther than the royal driveway. There were lights, lumber, doors, tools, bags of concrete, air vents, pipes, and at least four hundred things whose only purpose seemed to be making Dad say, “I could use one of those.”

Then I saw the gate.

To an ordinary shopper, it may have been a piece of metal fencing on display.

To me, it was the largest fence ever created by paws or mankind—a towering silver mountain demanding to be conquered.

I placed one paw on it.

Then another.

“Noodles,” Uncle Chris warned.

I climbed higher.

The overhead speaker crackled.

“Attention shoppers,” a voice announced. “Will someone please take care of the dog climbing the fence? Maybe hold her hand, since she is unruly.”

The entire aisle became quiet.

Dad turned toward the sound.

“I don’t know whose dog that is,” he said. “Mine is a Baroness. Very dignified.”

Uncle Chris lifted me down while trying not to laugh.

I permitted this only because I had already proven I could reach the third rail.

We continued to the concrete aisle. The bags were stacked like enormous gray pillows. Naturally, I crawled between them to inspect their structural integrity.

“If she comes out weighing eighty pounds more,” Dad said, “we are not paying for the concrete she’s wearing.”

Uncle Chris retrieved me again.

Next came the heating and air section. Large metal vents sat on the shelves like mysterious tunnels. I inserted my head into one.

My ears disappeared.

My dignity followed.

The vent amplified every breath into a dramatic storm.

“At least now we know the ductwork has a dog setting,” Dad said.

Then I found the tool aisle.

Rows of wrenches gleamed beneath the lights, but one called to me above all others: a heavy pipe wrench, almost as long as I was tall.

I seized it.

It was magnificent.

It was powerful.

It was extremely difficult to carry.

I ran anyway.

The pipe wrench swung from side to side while Uncle Chris hurried after me and Dad followed the sound of everyone laughing.

The overhead speaker crackled again.

“Attention shoppers. Can someone please take the wrench away from the dog? She can barely hold it and might drop it on someone’s foot.”

Dad stopped beside a display of paint buckets.

“If she fixes one pipe,” he called, “we’re putting her on payroll.”

I carried the wrench for nearly twelve heroic feet before Uncle Chris exchanged it for a small piece of beef jerky.

This was not surrender.

It was a negotiated royal trade agreement.

At checkout, Dad had the items from his list, Uncle Chris looked exhausted, and I had gathered valuable knowledge about fencing, concrete, ventilation, and large plumbing tools.

As we left, the voice on the speaker made one final announcement:

“The hardware department would like to thank the small dog for not purchasing the pipe wrench.”

Dad lifted his chai tea.

“Best store in the world,” he said.

I still disagreed.

But the beef jerky was acceptable, the gate had been a worthy opponent, and I had briefly controlled a pipe wrench.

For a working royal, it had been a productive day.
